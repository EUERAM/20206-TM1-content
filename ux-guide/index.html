UX foundations · 01

What is UX?

User experience (UX) is the overall experience a person has when interacting with a product, system, or service. It includes how well people can achieve their goals, how accessible and efficient the experience is, and how it makes them feel.

UX is not limited to screens. It includes the whole journey: discovering a service, using it, getting help, and understanding what happens next.

UX foundations · 02

UX vs UI: two connected disciplines

UX and UI work together, but they focus on different parts of an experience. UX shapes how the product works and whether it meets people's needs. UI shapes the visual and interactive surface through which people use it.

UX · Structure and experience

UX design focuses on how a product works, how efficiently people can use it, and whether the overall experience satisfies their needs.

Typical activities include user research, information architecture, navigation flows, usability testing, and wireframing.

Example: simplify buying a ticket by finding structural problems and reducing checkout from five screens to two.

UI · Visual and interactive surface

User interface design is the tangible point of visual communication and interaction between a person and a computer.

Typical activities include choosing colors, typography, sizes, shapes, menus, and the spatial layout of interface elements.

Example: design a high-contrast “Buy” button, choose readable type, and make the confirmation screen clear.

A beautiful interface cannot fix a confusing process, and a well-structured process still needs a clear interface. Good products bring UX and UI decisions together.

UX foundations · 03

Fallman's three ways of understanding design

Daniel Fallman describes three perspectives on design. They help us notice whether a project is being treated mainly as a problem to control, an opportunity for expression, or a process that balances people, context, and technology.

Conservative · Control and problem-solving

Design is a systematic, rational process similar to engineering. It prioritizes control, predictability, and solving a defined problem.

Example: a hospital X-ray interface must follow strict safety rules and behave predictably to help prevent critical errors.

Romantic · Expression and intuition

Design is a subjective, artistic activity guided by intuition and a strong creative vision.

Example: an immersive luxury-fashion campaign site might break familiar navigation conventions to create a striking visual emotion, even if it becomes less usable.

Pragmatic · Reflection and collaboration

Design is iterative, reflective, and collaborative. The designer mediates between the people, the context, and the available technology.

Example: a ride-hailing route map balances visual clarity, complex GPS technology, and the practical needs of both drivers and passengers.

These perspectives are not a ranking. Real projects often need all three: control where safety matters, expression where it helps communicate, and pragmatic iteration to bring the pieces together.

UX foundations · 04

Frameworks for moving from problem to solution

UX frameworks help teams organize their work. Each provides a different way to explore a problem, make decisions, and learn from a solution.

Design Thinking

A five-phase, people-centered framework: empathize to understand the problem, define it, ideate, prototype, and test. Teams can revisit earlier phases as they learn.

Double Diamond

Alternates divergent exploration with convergent selection: discover the problem, define it, develop possible solutions, and deliver a chosen direction.

Lean UX

Uses short, collaborative cycles instead of heavy documentation: build the smallest useful version, measure real user response, learn, and iterate.

A process from abstract to concrete

Jesse James Garrett's five planes

Jesse James Garrett's model organizes UX design into five connected planes. The process moves from abstract decisions toward concrete ones: strategy, requirements, structure, skeleton, and surface. Each plane builds on the questions answered in the one below it.

Garrett's planes · 01

Discovery · Research and strategy

Start by asking what the project needs to achieve and what the users need. Meaningful solutions begin with understanding people's goals, interests, and context.

Debrief

Bring the team onto the same page by recording objectives, conclusions, measures of success, and unanswered questions.

Desk research

Learn from existing resources such as articles, books, reports, and documentaries. Search broadly, then assess how relevant and reliable each source is.

Questionnaires and interviews

Turn the questions that remain after initial research into questionnaires and direct conversations with people.

Benchmarking · State of the art

Examine other tools and services people use for similar purposes. Notice the expectations and conventions they have learned.

Personas

Summarize research in accessible user profiles. Base personas on evidence from research, not assumptions or stereotypes.

Example: A persona for Mark, a 36-year-old resident of Los Angeles, might summarize his goals and the app features he prefers. Those details should come from research, rather than being invented to make the profile feel complete.

Garrett's planes · 02

Definition · Requirements and opportunities

Turn strategy into formal requirements: decide which characteristics the product needs. This abstract phase moves from research findings toward opportunities and plausible solutions.

Brainstorming

Share and make sense of research findings, identify patterns, and explore possible solutions together.

User scenarios

Tell a story about how a person interacts with the product. Scenarios reveal which features are needed and what requirements the team may have missed.

Example: Describe Jonathan's first use of an app: from installing it through the app store, to understanding his goals, to exploring the brand's video. The story helps identify the information and steps he needs along the way.

Garrett's planes · 03

Structure · Organization and behavior

Define the project's scope and how its parts fit together and behave. Structure turns requirements into diagrams and flows that make the experience coherent.

Information architecture

Organize content so people can find it naturally. A confusing structure is like putting oranges beside toothpaste: items that do not belong together become difficult to locate.

Example: map a landing page and its subpages, such as About Us, Opening Hours, Gallery, and Press.

User flows

Connect the steps in an experience and show how people move from one part to another. Use blocks, decisions, and conditions to make possible paths clear.

Example: map the route from a game's pause screen to restarting the game or reading its legal terms.

Card sorting

Ask people to sort information cards into groups. Their groupings can reveal a useful basis for navigation and the site's information architecture.

Conventions

People already know many established patterns. Use familiar conventions to help them, because changing existing habits takes effort.

Error handling

Prevent errors where possible, but plan for them to happen. Explain what went wrong and give people a clear way to recover and continue.

Garrett's planes · 04

Skeleton · The interface blueprint

Make the structure concrete by deciding which components let people use the finished product. The skeleton is the blueprint for the interface.

Interaction design

Consider how people interact with the product. New devices can bring new forms of interaction; do not design only for an isolated clickable screen.

Navigation design

Help people move through content easily and intuitively, so they can understand where they are and feel confident choosing what to do next.

Sketching

Try several screen layouts and interface concepts quickly. Early on, prioritize quantity over polish; then review, select, and iterate toward quality. Share sketches with the team to get feedback.

Example: sketch different blog app layouts to explore how images, articles, and a share icon might fit together.

Wireframes

Bring the skeleton's elements together into a blueprint that designers and developers can use to understand each screen. Focus on communication, annotate clearly, consider each screen's goals and real content, and reuse master elements where appropriate.

Garrett's planes · 05

Surface · The visual experience

The surface is the final plane, where the work comes together visually. It defines what the finished product looks like: visual hierarchy, color, typography, imagery, and the details of the interface.

The surface is more than decoration. Its visual choices should communicate the structure, support the user's goals, and make important actions easy to recognize.

Psychology for designers

UX laws and cognitive principles

These principles describe common patterns in how people perceive, decide, and act. Use them as prompts for design decisions, then test whether those decisions work for the people and context in your project.

Jakob's Law · Familiarity

People spend much of their time using other products. They expect a new system to work in familiar ways.

Example: a magnifying-glass icon usually means search, and a website logo at the upper left commonly links to the home page.

Hick's Law · Decision time

The time and mental effort needed to make a decision tend to grow as the number of choices increases.

Example: a focused search page with one clear search field can make it easier to begin than a page crowded with competing links and panels.

Fitts's Law · Target accessibility

The time and effort needed to reach an interactive target depend in part on its size and its distance from the current pointer or touch position.

Example: make a frequently used mobile action large enough to tap reliably and place it where a thumb can reach it.

Gestalt · Proximity

People tend to perceive objects close to each other as belonging to the same group.

Example: place a product's price directly below its image and use spacing to distinguish it from the next product.

Gestalt · Similarity

People tend to group objects that share visual characteristics such as size, shape, color, or behavior.

Example: if links share a consistent visual treatment, people can recognize them as having a similar function.

Zeigarnik effect · Unfinished tasks

People tend to remember unfinished or interrupted tasks more readily than completed ones.

Example: a course progress indicator can show what remains and help learners decide where to continue.

UX laws are useful starting points, not substitutes for research. Consider accessibility, the task, and the people using the product; validate important decisions with testing.
